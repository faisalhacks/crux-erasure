# Related work (draft, target <= 0.75 page)

All keys are in `report/refs.bib` and VERIFIED in `report/references_verified.md`. Wording follows decisions.md D4/D5. Never "first on erasure".

**The original study found the opposite of erasure, on a different measure.** Tessler et al. [CITE:tessler2024habermas] report that, although support for the majority position rose after deliberation, the Habermas Machine "demonstrably incorporated minority critiques into revised statements", and that revised statements overweighted the minority on an embedding stance axis (0.36 against an empirical share of 0.29). We take this finding seriously. Their measure is a position on an agree-disagree axis, which cannot tell a statement that names a disagreement from a vague mid-point statement. They also report that rankings, endorsement and quality ratings were affected by statement length, and the system picks its winner by aggregating per-participant rankings. They did not test whether rankings favour statements that hide disagreement. We ask that question; we do not claim their result is wrong. We cite their main text only.

**Content of AI consensus statements.** Mahajan et al. [CITE:mahajan2026commitments] find that LLM mediator prompts seeking agreement cover minority positions much less often than prompts asking for differences; minority coverage was judged by LLM judges only. Wan et al. [CITE:wan2026deliberative] show that LLM agents "can agree more while knowing less", and Zhu et al. [CITE:zhu2025voice] benchmark 18 LLMs and report minority under-representation. Zhu et al. also find that off-the-shelf LLM judges' correlations with human ratings "never exceed 0.4", which is why we validate our scorer against blind human labels (gate 1) instead of assuming it. None of these tests human rankings of competing statements. A conceptual critique argues that the Habermas Machine fails Habermas's own criteria for legitimate discourse [CITE:farrow2026habermas] (abstract-level claim only).

**Measuring omission.** Zaghouani [CITE:zaghouani2026accountable] proposes an embedding formula also called "Erasure", with no validation and no ranking study. We therefore call ours the "crux erasure score". Coverage and fairness metrics for summaries [CITE:zhang2024fairsumm; CITE:khosravani2024argument; CITE:shandilya2020fairness] are the closest measurement precedents.

**Bridging and consensus selection.** Bridging-based ranking, as in Community Notes [CITE:wojcik2022birdwatch] and "bridging systems" [CITE:ovadya2023bridging], promotes items endorsed across groups that usually disagree. Related studies warn that such selection can disfavour contested content: a naive bridging ranker downranked URLs on controversial topics [CITE:milli2024bridging] (NeurIPS 2024 workshop paper), and quality-based comment ranking may reduce the visibility of legitimate viewpoints [CITE:revel2025representative]. Comparing opinion-selection algorithms, Hafid et al. caution that consensus-seeking selection can flatten conflicting preferences and erase minority voices [CITE:hafid2026opinion]. Generative social choice [CITE:fish2023generative] returns a representative slate and measures felt representation. These works study URLs, comments, opinions or slates, not participants' ranking of candidate group statements.

**Sycophancy.** Human preference data can favour responses that match a user's views over truthful ones [CITE:sharma2024sycophancy]. A further study reports that RLHF raised persuasiveness without raising correctness [CITE:wen2025mislead], but an ICLR 2026 Blog Track rebuttal argues its results may stem largely from bugs in the RLHF setup, so we use it only as motivation and rest on Sharma et al. We frame crux erasure as the group analogue of sycophancy. The framing is ours; we found no source stating it. It differs in one respect: no single rater is flattered. Each is partly pleased by a statement that does not name the clash. Whether rankings show this is the empirical question.

**Gap.** To our knowledge, this is the first test of whether participants' rankings of candidate consensus statements favour statements that hide the group's disagreement. Prior work did not link what participants reward to disagreement-hiding content. Our search was keyword-based, and for several cited works we read only abstracts or partial text.
